# Pneumonia: a lung infection that needs assessment

Pneumonia is inflammation of the lungs, usually caused by infection. The NHS says some people need antibiotics, and babies, older people and those with heart or lung conditions are at risk of becoming seriously ill and may need hospital treatment.

Symptoms can start suddenly or build over a few days: a cough, shortness of breath, a high temperature, chest pain, an aching body, feeling very tired, loss of appetite, wheezing (babies may grunt), and confusion, which is common in older people.

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 if you have had a cough for up to three weeks; you are coughing up yellow or green mucus; you have chest pain that comes and goes or happens when breathing or coughing; or you are short of breath.

Call emergency services or go to A&E if you are struggling to breathe (choking, gasping, unable to speak); you have pale, blue or blotchy skin, lips or tongue; you are coughing up blood; you suddenly feel confused, for example not knowing where you are; or you cannot wake a baby or they feel floppy.

Treatment and recovery, in general terms: pneumonia is usually caused by a bacterial or viral infection, and bacterial cases are usually treated with antibiotics prescribed by a clinician. Depending on symptoms, most people recover in 2 to 4 weeks. Hospital care may be needed for older adults, young children, people with heart or lung conditions, and anyone very unwell. If given antibiotics at home, finish the course even when feeling better, and contact the doctor if there is no improvement after 2 to 3 days. Helpful habits: rest, drink plenty of fluids, cover coughs and sneezes and wash hands. The NHS advises not taking cough medicine, because coughing helps clear the infection, and not smoking.

Reducing risk: vaccines exist against some infections that can lead to pneumonia, including pneumococcal, flu, RSV and COVID-19 vaccines, recommended for particular age and risk groups. A clinician or pharmacist can say which apply.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: fever, breathlessness and chest pain together are a reason to contact a clinician today. A wearable showing a raised resting heart rate or reduced activity is not a diagnosis. Not medical advice.
