# Hay fever: pollen allergy

Hay fever is a common allergy causing sneezing, coughing and itchy eyes. The NHS says it cannot be cured or prevented, but you can ease symptoms, and medicines can help.

Symptoms: sneezing and coughing; a runny or blocked nose; itchy, red or watery eyes; itchy throat, mouth, nose and ears; loss of smell; pain around the sides of the head and forehead; headache; and tiredness. Symptoms are usually worse between late March and September, especially when it is warm, humid and windy, which is when the pollen count is highest. Hay fever can last weeks or months, unlike a cold, which usually goes within one to two weeks.

Things to do when the pollen count is high (from the NHS): put petroleum jelly around the nostrils to trap pollen; wear wraparound sunglasses, a mask or a wide-brimmed hat; shower and change clothes after being outside; keep windows and doors shut where possible; avoid drying washing outdoors; vacuum and dust with a damp cloth; and avoid cutting grass or spending time in grassy places. A pharmacist can recommend over-the-counter treatments without a prescription.

Things to avoid: do not smoke or be around smoke, do not dry clothes outside, and do not keep fresh flowers indoors.

See a GP if your symptoms are getting worse or do not improve after pharmacy treatments. A GP might offer a steroid-based treatment and, if other options do not work, may refer for immunotherapy, which gradually builds tolerance to pollen.

Relevance to sleep and activity: nasal congestion and itching can disturb sleep and make outdoor exercise less pleasant in high-pollen weather. Indoor options or exercise at lower-pollen times may help.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: link a poor-sleep run to pollen season only as a possibility, never as a conclusion. Wheeze or breathlessness is not typical for hay fever alone and should be checked. Not medical advice.
