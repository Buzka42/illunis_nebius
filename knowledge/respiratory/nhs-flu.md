# Flu: symptoms, spread and when to get help

Flu often gets better by itself but can make some people seriously ill. The NHS encourages people to have the flu vaccine if they are advised to.

Symptoms come on quickly and can include a sudden high temperature, aching body, tiredness or exhaustion, a dry cough, sore throat, headache, difficulty sleeping and loss of appetite. Children may also have ear pain, sore eyes or swollen glands and seem less active.

Cold versus flu: they are similar, but flu appears within hours, affects more than the nose and throat, and makes you feel exhausted and too unwell to carry on as normal. A cold builds gradually over a day or two, mostly affects the nose and throat, and you usually still feel able to do normal things.

Self-care: rest, drink plenty of fluids, and stay at home until you feel better. Flu is very infectious, and you are most likely to pass it on in the first five days. It spreads through coughs and sneezes, and germs can live on hands and surfaces for 24 hours, so washing hands, using tissues and binning them help.

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111 if you or your child have flu symptoms and you are worried about a baby or child; you are 65 or over; you are pregnant or recently gave birth; you have a long-term condition such as diabetes or a heart, lung, liver, kidney or nerve condition; you have a weakened immune system; you feel very unwell or short of breath; or symptoms do not improve after 7 days.

Call emergency services or go to A&E for sudden chest pain, severe difficulty breathing, or coughing up blood.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: a wearable showing higher resting heart rate and poorer sleep during an illness is a normal pattern. Suggest rest and fluids and flag the warning signs, without interpreting numbers. Not medical advice.
