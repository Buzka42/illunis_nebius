# Asthma: symptoms, triggers and attacks

Asthma is a common condition that affects breathing. The NHS says it cannot currently be cured, but when well treated people should not have problems with symptoms.

Main symptoms: wheezing, coughing, shortness of breath and a tight chest. They can be mild or severe, usually come and go, and may be worse at night and early in the morning. An asthma attack means severe symptoms with difficulty breathing, and it can be life-threatening.

Common triggers: exercise, high air pollution, cold air, and contact with things you are allergic to, such as pollen, dust, mould or animals. Asthma usually starts in childhood but can begin at any age.

Diagnosis: a GP or nurse asks questions and listens to the chest, and may arrange a simple breathing test and sometimes a blood test. A handheld peak flow meter may be lent to use at home for a few weeks to check how well the lungs are working.

If someone has asthma, they usually have a personal asthma action plan from their clinician, and during an attack they should follow that plan: sit upright, try to stay calm, and use their prescribed reliever as directed. This knowledge bank does not give medicine amounts.

Call emergency services if someone is having an asthma attack and feels worse at any time, does not feel better after using their reliever as directed in their plan, or does not have an inhaler. Do not drive to hospital; the call handler will advise. After any attack, even if recovered, ask for an urgent GP appointment within two days to review treatment and prevent another.

See a GP if you or your child have asthma symptoms; if treatment is not helping or the reliever is needed more often; if symptoms are stopping usual activities or waking you at night; or if you need advice about using your inhaler.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: wheezing, breathlessness or exercise-linked cough that is new or worsening is a reason to see a clinician. Do not give treatment advice. Not medical advice.
