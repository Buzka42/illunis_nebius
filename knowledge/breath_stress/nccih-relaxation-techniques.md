# Relaxation techniques and the relaxation response

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health describes relaxation techniques as practices that bring about the body's "relaxation response": slower breathing, lower blood pressure and a lower heart rate. It is the opposite of the stress response.

Common types:

- **Progressive muscle relaxation**: tense different muscle groups in turn, then release the tension.
- **Autogenic training**: a series of mental exercises using self-suggested ideas of warmth and heaviness to focus on the body's relaxation.
- **Guided imagery (visualisation)**: picture calm scenes or events and try to produce a similar feeling in the body.
- **Biofeedback-assisted relaxation**: a device shows heart rate, blood pressure or muscle tension so you learn how your body responds to stress or calm.
- **Self-hypnosis**: learning to produce relaxation when prompted by your own cue or phrase.
- **Breathing exercises**: slow, deep, diaphragmatic breathing.

Practices such as massage, meditation, yoga, tai chi and qigong can also produce the relaxation response, but are covered in separate fact sheets. The fact sheet also notes that some studies compare relaxation techniques with cognitive behavioural therapy, a talking treatment that helps people spot automatic, unhelpful thinking.

In a US survey, about 6 percent of adults reported using relaxation techniques in the previous year. They are generally considered safe for healthy people, and most studies report no negative effects. Occasionally people report more anxiety, intrusive thoughts or fear of losing control, and there are rare reports that some techniques may worsen symptoms in people with epilepsy, certain psychiatric conditions, or a history of abuse or trauma.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: when a user wants to unwind, offer a choice (a breathing minute, tensing and releasing muscles from feet upward, or imagining a calm place) and let them pick. These are tools for everyday stress, not treatment for a medical or mental health condition. Ongoing distress should lead to a clinician.
