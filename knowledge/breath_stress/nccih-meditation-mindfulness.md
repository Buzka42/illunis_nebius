# Meditation and mindfulness: what the evidence says about safety

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health explains that "meditation" covers many practices that join mind and body to calm the mind and support wellbeing. Some focus attention on a sensation, such as breathing, a sound, an image or a repeated word. Mindfulness keeps attention on the present moment without judging it. Programs such as mindfulness-based stress reduction combine meditation with group discussion and strategies for applying it to stressful situations, and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy mixes mindfulness with elements of cognitive behavioural therapy.

Safety. These practices are usually considered to carry few risks, but few studies have looked for harms, so firm statements are not possible. A 2020 review of 83 studies with about 6,700 participants found that roughly 8 percent of people had a negative effect, a rate similar to that reported for psychological therapies. The most commonly reported negative effects were anxiety and depression. In a small analysis of mindfulness-based stress reduction programs, the practices were not more harmful than no treatment.

Popularity. In a large US survey, the share of adults who meditated more than doubled between 2002 and 2022, from 7.5 to 17.3 percent, making it the most popular of the complementary approaches measured.

What this means for general use: most people can try short practices, but some find that sitting with their thoughts brings up discomfort. It is reasonable to stop, change the practice, or take a break.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: describe meditation as something many people find calming, with honest caveats, never as a cure. If a user reports increasing anxiety, low mood, or distressing experiences when meditating, suggest pausing and speaking with a clinician or the mental health expert.
