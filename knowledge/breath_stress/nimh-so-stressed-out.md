# Stress or anxiety? A US National Institute of Mental Health fact sheet

NIMH separates the two ideas. **Stress** is the physical or mental response to an external cause, such as an exam, a life change or an illness. It can be short term or repeated over time, and usually eases when the situation is resolved. It can be helpful, like a deadline that spurs action, or harmful, like losing sleep. **Anxiety** is the body's reaction to stress and can persist even without an immediate threat. It tends to involve a lasting feeling of apprehension that interferes with daily life.

Both can cause excessive worry, uneasiness, tension, headaches or body pain, high blood pressure and sleep loss. If anxiety does not go away, it may affect sleep and the immune, digestive, cardiovascular and reproductive systems and raise the risk of conditions such as anxiety disorders and depression.

Signs that it may be time for more support, as the sheet puts it: you feel unable to manage the stress, and symptoms interfere with everyday life, make you avoid things, or seem always present.

Coping ideas from the sheet: learn what triggers your stress and what helps you, with some trial and error; use a relaxation or mindfulness app; exercise and eat regular healthy meals; keep a sleep routine; limit excess caffeine; notice and challenge unhelpful thoughts; reach out to friends or family who help you cope.

When to get help: if you are struggling or symptoms will not go away, talking to a professional is a good idea. Talking therapy and medication are the two main treatments for anxiety, and many people benefit from both. If you are in immediate distress or thinking about hurting yourself, contact local emergency or crisis services, such as the 988 Lifeline in the United States.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: reflect the difference gently, avoid diagnosing, and offer coping choices. Crisis language must go to crisis resources, not to coping tips.
