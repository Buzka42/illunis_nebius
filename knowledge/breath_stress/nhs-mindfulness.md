# Mindfulness: paying attention to the present moment

The NHS describes mindfulness as paying attention, moment by moment, to what is happening inside you (thoughts, feelings, body sensations) and around you (sights, sounds, smells, tastes). It is easy to rush through life "in our heads", out of touch with our bodies. Reconnecting can be as simple as noticing the feel of a banister on the stairs.

How it is said to help wellbeing:

- We notice things we had been taking for granted, which can make daily life richer.
- We become aware of the stream of thoughts and feelings and see how we can get tangled in it unhelpfully. Stepping back lets us see patterns and recognise that thoughts are mental events that do not have to control us.
- It can help us decide whether brooding about a problem is actually solving it.
- We may notice signs of stress or anxiety earlier and deal with them sooner.

The page notes that mindfulness-based therapies are recommended by the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) as a way to treat less severe depression, and NICE also recommends that employers make mindfulness available to staff for wellbeing at work. The therapy use is clinical, under professional guidance, and separate from everyday practice.

Everyday ways to practise include: noticing sensations during routine activities, paying attention while eating or walking, a few minutes of quiet breathing, and noticing when the mind has drifted and gently returning to the present.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: suggest one tiny practice (for example, noticing five things you can hear) and keep the tone light. Mindfulness does not suit everyone at all times. If it brings up distress, a person can stop, and persistent low mood or anxiety is a reason to speak to a clinician or the mental health expert.
