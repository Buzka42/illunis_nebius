# Understanding everyday stress

Stress is the body's reaction to feeling threatened or under pressure. Everyone feels it at times, especially during change or life challenges such as money worries, work issues or relationship problems. A little stress can help us focus and get things done. The body releases adrenaline, the "fight or flight" hormone, which gives a short boost.

Too much stress, especially when it feels out of our control, can affect mood, body and relationships. Long-term or severe stress can lead to physical, mental and emotional exhaustion, often called burnout.

Signs to notice:

- **Feelings**: irritability, anger, tearfulness, worry, hopelessness, feeling overwhelmed, racing thoughts, trouble deciding.
- **Body**: stomach problems, tension headaches, muscle pain, skin reactions such as rashes or hives, feeling dizzy, sick or faint. Stress can raise blood pressure and cause chest pains for a while, but the page is clear that worrying symptoms should be checked.
- **Behaviour**: changes in eating or exercise, drinking, smoking or using substances more, and avoiding people or things once enjoyed.

What causes stress varies from person to person, and the level that feels comfortable differs. Stress tends to arise when we feel we lack the resources to meet a challenge. The first step in handling it is knowing how it affects you and why.

The page links to practical relief ideas and a personal self-care plan, and suggests seeing a GP if symptoms worry you or stress feels severe.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: reflect back patterns the user mentions, such as sleep, energy and mood across the week, as observations, and offer a small relief step. Avoid labelling anyone as "burned out". Chest pain, severe symptoms or thoughts of self-harm need a clinician or crisis support, and the assistant should say so.
