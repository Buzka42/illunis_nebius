# Acne

Acne is a common skin condition causing spots, from mild (a few spots) to severe (deep, painful lumps under the skin). It usually affects the face, chest and back. The NHS lists symptoms such as oily skin, spots, large and sometimes painful lumps, colour or texture changes or scarring, and skin that may feel hot or painful. Acne can also affect mental health, causing anxiety, depression and low self-esteem.

Types of spots include whiteheads, blackheads, papules (raised bumps), pustules (bumps filled with pus), and larger nodules and cysts. A person may have more than one type.

Self-care tips from the page: wash your skin twice a day with a gentle cleanser; do not pick or squeeze spots, as this can scar; and do not scrub the skin, which can irritate it and make acne worse. A pharmacist can suggest products available without prescription.

See a GP if you have tried treating it yourself and it is not getting better; you have lots of spots or they are deep, painful or causing scars; you have signs of a skin infection such as hot, swollen, red skin (redness can be harder to see on brown and black skin); or it is affecting your mental health. A GP may offer prescription treatments, stronger than those from pharmacies and shops and very effective for more severe acne, or refer to a skin specialist.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: acknowledge that visible skin conditions can affect mood and confidence, and gently suggest that help is available. Do not recommend specific medicines or claim foods cause acne. If acne is linked with low mood or distress, mention the mental health options. Not medical advice.
