# Contact dermatitis: a skin reaction to something you touch

Contact dermatitis is a type of eczema triggered by contact with a particular substance. The NHS says it usually improves or clears completely if the trigger is identified and avoided.

Symptoms: itchy, blistered, dry and cracked skin. Lighter skin can become red, and darker skin can become dark brown, purple or grey. The reaction usually occurs within hours or days of exposure to an irritant or allergen and most commonly affects the hands and face.

Practical steps: speak to a pharmacist if it is troubling you, since they can recommend emollients (moisturisers) to stop the skin drying. See a GP if symptoms are persistent, recurrent or severe. A GP can try to identify the cause, and may refer you to a skin specialist if the substance cannot be identified or symptoms are not responding.

Prevention, as the page describes: avoid contact with the allergens or irritants causing symptoms. If you cannot avoid contact: rinse the affected skin with warm water and an emollient as soon as possible; use gloves to protect your hands but take them off now and then, because sweating can worsen symptoms (cotton gloves under rubber gloves can help if rubber irritates); change irritating products, checking the ingredients in make-up and soap; and apply emollients frequently and generously, using emollient soap substitutes instead of regular soap.

Examples of triggers named in the NHS eczema pages include soaps, detergents, some fabrics and metals, but a clinician is the right person to help identify an individual cause.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: a diary of new products, jobs and symptom timing can help narrow down triggers. It should not say which substance caused a reaction. Swelling of the face or lips, or difficulty breathing, after contact with a substance is an emergency. Not medical advice.
