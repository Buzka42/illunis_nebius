# Moles: what is normal and what to get checked

Moles are small, coloured spots on the skin. Most people have them and they are usually nothing to worry about unless they change size, shape or colour.

Typical harmless moles are round or oval with a smooth edge. They can be flat or raised, smooth or rough, sometimes with a hair growing from them, and are usually darker on brown and black skin. It is normal for babies to be born with moles, for new moles to appear (especially in children and teenagers), for moles to fade or disappear with age, and for moles to darken slightly during pregnancy.

Signs a mole could be cancerous, as the NHS puts it: some moles can be a sign of melanoma, a type of skin cancer. Melanomas may change colour over time or have more than two colours, often have uneven edges, and sometimes bleed, itch, or become crusty or raised.

See a GP if a mole has changed size, shape or colour; is painful or itchy; is inflamed, bleeding or crusty; or if you have a new or unusual mark on the skin that has not gone away after a few weeks. Finding a melanoma early can make it easier to treat. If a GP thinks a mole may be melanoma, you should be referred to a hospital specialist, with an appointment expected within two weeks. The main treatment for melanoma is surgery to remove the mole.

Harmless moles are not usually treated on the NHS, though cosmetic removal can be arranged privately.

Prevention link: protecting skin from sunburn is the main general step to lower skin cancer risk (see the sun safety note).

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: a camera or app cannot judge a mole. The assistant should encourage the user to have any changing mole looked at by a clinician and should not reassure or diagnose. Not medical advice.
