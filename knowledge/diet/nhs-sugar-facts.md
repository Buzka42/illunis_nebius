# Sugar: free sugars and how much is a lot

Eating a lot of sugar can lead to weight gain and tooth decay. The sugars to watch are called **free sugars**: sugars added to food or drink (in biscuits, chocolate, flavoured yoghurt, cereal, fizzy drinks), plus sugars in honey, syrups, and in unsweetened fruit juice, vegetable juice and smoothies. Sugar found naturally inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk does not count as free sugar, and there is no need to cut it down, though it is included in the "total sugars" figure on labels.

UK guidance is that free sugars should make up no more than about 5 percent of daily energy. For adults that is roughly 30 g a day, about seven sugar cubes. For children it is lower: about 24 g at ages 7 to 10, 19 g at 4 to 6, 14 g at 2 to 3 and 10 g at age 1. Babies under one have no limit set, but sugar should not be added to their food or drinks. A single can of fizzy cola can hold more than the adult daily amount.

Practical ideas: choose water or unsweetened drinks, look at labels for "total sugars" per 100 g, pick plain yoghurt and add fruit, and treat cakes, sweets and biscuits as occasional foods. Frequency matters for teeth as well as amount, so sipping sugary drinks over a long time is harder on teeth than one drink with a meal.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: describe sugar in terms of patterns ("a sweet drink most afternoons") and offer one swap, without moral language about foods. Questions about blood sugar and diabetes belong with the endocrinology expert and a clinician.
