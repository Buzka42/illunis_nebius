# Fish and shellfish in a balanced diet

A balanced diet should include at least two portions of fish a week, one of which is oily. Most people in the UK eat less fish than that, and less oily fish in particular.

Types and what they offer:

- **Oily fish** such as salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring, trout, pilchards and sprats. They are rich in long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, which may help protect against heart disease, and are a good source of vitamin D. Canned oily fish with soft bones, such as sardines and pilchards, also provide calcium and phosphorus. Fresh and canned tuna do not count as oily fish.
- **White fish** such as cod, haddock and pollock are low in fat and can replace red or processed meat, which are usually higher in saturated fat.
- **Shellfish** such as prawns, mussels, scallops and squid are low in fat and supply minerals such as selenium, zinc, iodine and copper. Mussels, oysters, squid and crab also contain some omega-3, though less than oily fish.

Cooking matters: steamed, baked or grilled fish is a better choice than fried, especially in batter. Eating a variety of species helps keep fish stocks healthy.

Advice differs in pregnancy, while breastfeeding and for children, who have limits on certain fish. A wellbeing assistant should not give those details and should point users to official pregnancy guidance or a clinician.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: for someone who rarely eats fish, a tin of sardines or mackerel on toast once a week is an easy start. Allergy or intolerance questions need a clinician.
