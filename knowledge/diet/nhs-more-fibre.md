# Fibre: how much, and easy ways to eat more

UK guidance suggests adults aim for about 30 g of fibre a day, as part of a balanced diet. Most adults eat roughly 20 g, so the common gap is about a third. Younger people need less: around 15 g at ages 2 to 5, 20 g at 5 to 11, and 25 g at 11 to 16.

Why it matters. Diets rich in fibre are linked with a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer. Fibre also supports digestion and helps prevent constipation. These are associations seen in populations, not a promise for one person.

Practical ways to add fibre, in the page's spirit:

- Choose higher-fibre breakfast cereals or porridge, since oats are a good source.
- Pick wholemeal or granary bread, or higher-fibre white bread, and wholegrains such as brown rice, bulgur or wholewheat pasta.
- Eat potatoes with the skin on.
- Stir pulses (beans, lentils, chickpeas) into stews, curries and salads.
- Add vegetables to meals as sides or in sauces.
- Fruit, fresh or dried, helps, though dried fruit is sticky and best eaten with meals rather than grazed on, to protect teeth.

Variety matters: getting fibre from many foods is better than leaning on one. Increasing fibre gradually and drinking enough fluid generally helps the gut adjust, since a sudden jump can cause wind or bloating.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: if a user says they want to eat better, a single extra fibre-rich food at one meal is a small, concrete step. If digestion changes persistently, that is a topic for the digestion expert and a clinician, not a reason to adjust diet alone.
