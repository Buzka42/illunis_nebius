# Dietary fat: saturated and unsaturated

Some fat is part of a healthy diet. It supplies essential fatty acids that the body cannot make, and it helps the body absorb the fat-soluble vitamins A, D and E. But all fat is energy-dense: a gram provides 9 kcal, against 4 kcal for a gram of carbohydrate or protein.

Two broad groups:

- **Saturated fats** come mostly from animal sources and a few plant ones. Examples include fatty meat, sausages and pies, butter, ghee and lard, hard cheese, cream and ice cream, many biscuits, cakes and pastries, chocolate, palm oil and coconut oil. A diet high in saturated fat can raise cholesterol, which raises the risk of heart disease.
- **Unsaturated fats** are found in vegetable, rapeseed, olive and sunflower oils, oily fish, nuts and seeds. UK advice is to replace some saturated fat with unsaturated fat, and to cut down on fat overall, rather than adding "good" fat on top.

Most foods contain a mixture. The practical guidance is to eat less of the foods high in saturated fat and trans fat, and use small amounts of unsaturated oils.

Everyday swaps that fit the guidance: choosing leaner meat or cutting visible fat, grilling or baking instead of frying, using a little oil instead of butter, choosing lower-fat dairy, and having a handful of unsalted nuts instead of a pastry.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: talk in terms of patterns and swaps, not forbidden foods. Anyone with a heart or cholesterol concern should take food-fat questions to a clinician or dietitian, and a cardiology expert may add context.
