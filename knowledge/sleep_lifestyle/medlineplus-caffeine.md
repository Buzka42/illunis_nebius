# Caffeine: effects and when to be careful

Caffeine is a bitter substance found naturally in coffee beans, tea leaves, kola nuts and cacao pods, and added to some medicines, energy drinks and snacks. Most people take it in drinks. Typical amounts reported by MedlinePlus: about 95 to 200 mg in an 8-ounce cup of coffee, 35 to 45 mg in a 12-ounce can of cola, 70 to 100 mg in an 8-ounce energy drink, and 14 to 60 mg in a cup of tea.

Effects on the body: it stimulates the central nervous system, so you may feel more awake and energetic; it is a mild diuretic, so you pass more urine; it increases stomach acid, which can lead to upset stomach or heartburn; it may interfere with calcium absorption; and it raises blood pressure. Levels peak within about an hour, and effects can last four to six hours. That timing is why afternoon and evening caffeine can disturb sleep.

Energy drinks carry large amounts of caffeine and often sugar. There is limited evidence they improve alertness briefly, and the page warns they can be dangerous. Mixing them with alcohol is dangerous because caffeine can hide how drunk you are.

Check with a health provider about limiting caffeine if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have sleep disorders such as insomnia, migraines or other chronic headaches, anxiety, reflux or ulcers, an irregular heartbeat, high blood pressure, or take certain medicines or supplements.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: if sleep or restlessness is a concern, suggest trying the last caffeinated drink earlier in the day and note the effect over a week. Present it as an experiment the user controls. Palpitations, chest pain or severe anxiety need a clinician.
