# Six habits for better sleep ("sleep hygiene")

The NHS says many people struggle with sleep at some point, and small changes to habits and routines can make a real difference over time. Six ideas:

1. **Keep a regular routine.** Going to bed and getting up at similar times each day, including days off, helps the body know when to sleep.
2. **Relax before bed.** A calm wind-down helps. The page suggests avoiding phones, tablets and computers for an hour before bed because screen light can make it harder to fall asleep. Reading, meditation, breathing exercises, calming music or a podcast are alternatives.
3. **Manage worries.** Talking to someone you trust or writing worries down helps. If you worry in bed, make a list of tomorrow's tasks before sleep to clear the mind.
4. **Watch food, drink and nicotine.** Large meals near bedtime can make it harder to fall asleep. Caffeine keeps you more awake, so a warm milky or herbal drink may be better in the evening. Alcohol and nicotine can disrupt sleep, so avoid them for 1 to 2 hours before bed.
5. **Make the bedroom suitable.** Quiet, dark and cool suits many people: earplugs, closed curtains, an eye mask, a cooler room, clocks out of view, or soft background sound.
6. **Do not force sleep.** If still awake after about 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in a comfortable place, and return to bed when sleepy.

Activity helps too, even small amounts, but if exercise close to bedtime disturbs your sleep, move it earlier in the day.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: if a user's sleep ratings or wearable sleep time have dropped, offer one habit from the list rather than all six, and ask what feels realistic. Describe patterns as observations, never as a diagnosis. Long-lasting sleep trouble is covered in the insomnia note and warrants a clinician.
