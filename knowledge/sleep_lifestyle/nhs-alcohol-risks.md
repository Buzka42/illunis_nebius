# Alcohol: low-risk drinking guidance

NHS advice for keeping risk low: do not regularly drink more than 14 units a week, spread drinking over three or more days if you reach that amount, and have several drink-free days each week. Units depend on drink size and strength. If you are pregnant or could become pregnant, the safest approach is not to drink.

The page stresses that "low risk" does not mean "safe": there is no safe drinking level. Newer evidence on harm has strengthened, including links with several cancers, and the older idea that moderate drinking protects the heart is now considered less well supported. Drinking over 14 units a week for many years can raise the risk of mouth, throat and breast cancer, stroke, heart disease, liver disease and brain damage that affects memory and behaviour. Regular high-risk drinking is also linked with worse mental health, and strongly with self-harm and suicide.

For sleep and lifestyle: alcohol may help some people fall asleep quickly, but it tends to disrupt sleep patterns and reduce deep sleep, so people often feel less rested. Morning-after anxiety and low mood are common.

Practical ideas from the NHS cutting-down page: make a plan and a limit before you start, set a budget, tell friends and family, take it a day at a time, choose smaller or lower-strength drinks, eat first, alternate with water, and take drink-free days. Benefits many people notice quickly: better mornings, less daytime tiredness, more energy and easier weight management.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: if a user mentions drinking alongside poor sleep or low mood, note the link as an observation and offer one change, such as no alcohol in the last two hours before bed. If drinking feels hard to control, or the user is worried about withdrawal, suggest a GP or local alcohol support service.
