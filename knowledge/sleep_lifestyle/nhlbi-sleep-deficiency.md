# How too little sleep affects health

The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute explains that getting enough good-quality sleep at the right times helps protect mental and physical health, quality of life and safety.

Signs you may not be getting enough: feeling very tired in the day, not feeling refreshed on waking, and finding it hard to stay awake in quiet situations such as reading, watching TV, sitting in a meeting or a car, or after lunch. Sleep deficiency interferes with work, school, driving and social life.

Mind and mood: it affects learning, focus and reaction time. People can find it harder to make decisions, solve problems, remember things, manage emotions and cope with change, and they tend to make more mistakes and take longer. It has been linked with depression and risk-taking. Children and teens may be impulsive, moody or have trouble paying attention.

Body: good sleep supports the heart and blood vessels; helps balance the hormones that signal hunger and fullness, so lack of sleep can make you hungrier; affects how the body handles insulin and blood sugar; supports growth and tissue repair; and helps the body fight infection. Long-term sleep deficiency is linked with a higher risk of heart disease, high blood pressure, obesity, diabetes and stroke. Research cited found adults who regularly get 7 to 8 hours had a lower risk of obesity and high blood pressure.

Safety: losing even 1 to 2 hours a night for several nights can reduce function as if you had missed a day or two of sleep entirely. Microsleeps, brief unnoticed lapses while awake, are a danger when driving.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: link a run of short nights to how the user feels in neutral language, suggest one habit, and warn against driving when very sleepy. Persistent sleepiness, loud snoring or pauses in breathing are reasons to see a clinician.
