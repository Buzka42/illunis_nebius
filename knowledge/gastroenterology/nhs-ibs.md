# Irritable bowel syndrome: symptoms, triggers and self-help

IBS is a common condition of the digestive system, usually lifelong, in which symptoms come and go. The NHS lists the main symptoms as stomach pain or cramps (usually worse after eating and better after a poo), bloating, diarrhoea and constipation. There are better days and flare-ups. Other symptoms can include wind, mucus in the stool, tiredness, nausea, backache, urinary symptoms and pelvic pain.

Flare-ups sometimes have no obvious cause, but possible triggers are alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty food, stress and anxiety, and regular antibiotic use.

Self-help ideas, once a clinician has confirmed IBS: eat a healthy balanced diet; keep a food and symptom diary to spot triggers; drink enough fluid (the page suggests about 8 to 10 drinks a day, avoiding too much caffeine); find ways to relax; get regular exercise; and consider trying probiotics for a month. Avoid skipping meals, eating too fast, lots of fatty, spicy or processed food, more than three portions of fresh fruit a day, more than three caffeinated drinks, and heavy alcohol or fizzy drinks.

For bloating and wind the page suggests regular oats, a small amount of linseed, and avoiding hard-to-digest foods and certain sweeteners. For diarrhoea, cut down on high-fibre foods; for constipation, increase soluble fibre (oats, pulses, carrots, peeled potatoes) and fluids. A pharmacist can advise about over-the-counter options.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you think you have IBS and symptoms have lasted over four weeks, so other problems can be ruled out, or if diet changes are not helping or you need to avoid many foods. Ask for an urgent appointment or call NHS 111 if you have lost a lot of weight for no reason, bleeding from the bottom or bloody diarrhoea, a hard lump or swelling in your tummy, or breathlessness with palpitations and unusual paleness.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: encourage a gentle diary and stress relief, without diagnosing. Not medical advice.
