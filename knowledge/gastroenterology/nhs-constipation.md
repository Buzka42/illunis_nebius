# Constipation: causes, self-help and warning signs

Constipation means changes in how you poo: going less often or finding it hard. It is common and affects all ages. The NHS says it is likely if you have gone fewer than three times in a week or less often than usual, the poo is dry, hard, lumpy or unusually large or small, you strain or have pain, or you feel you have not fully emptied your bowels. A stomach ache, bloating or nausea may come with it. In older people or those with dementia or a learning disability it can be missed, so look for behaviour changes such as confusion.

Common causes: too little fibre or fluid, being inactive, ignoring the urge to go, changes in diet or routine, a side effect of some medicines, and stress, anxiety or depression. It is also common in pregnancy and after birth and more common over 65. Medical causes such as diverticular disease or bowel cancer are rare.

Self-help the NHS suggests: eat a balanced diet and gradually increase fibre (including oats, wheat bran or linseed, and fruit such as apples, apricots, grapes, raspberries and strawberries); drink plenty of water and avoid alcohol; keep a regular toilet routine and do not put off the urge; rest your feet on a low stool so your knees are above your hips; and be more active, since a daily walk can help. These measures are safe in pregnancy and can take days to weeks to work. If they do not help, a pharmacist can advise on short-term options.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if constipation is not improving, is regular, comes with regular bloating, blood in the poo, unexplained weight loss, constant tiredness or tummy pain, if you notice a sudden change in bowel habits, or if a medicine may be causing it. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without speaking to the doctor.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: link fibre, fluid, movement and routine, and avoid advice on laxatives. Not medical advice.
