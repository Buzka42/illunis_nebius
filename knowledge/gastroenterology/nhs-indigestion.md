# Indigestion: usually harmless, sometimes a flag

Most people have indigestion (dyspepsia) at some point, and the NHS says it is usually not a sign of anything more serious and can be treated at home. Symptoms after eating or drinking include heartburn (a burning feeling in the chest), feeling full and bloated, nausea, burping, wind, and bringing up food or bitter fluid. Stomach ache or back pain usually point to constipation, not indigestion.

Typical causes: stomach acid irritating the stomach lining or throat (acid reflux). It can be caused or worsened by foods and drinks such as coffee, tomatoes, alcohol, chocolate, fatty or spicy food; being overweight; smoking; pregnancy; stress and anxiety; some medicines, including anti-inflammatory painkillers; a hiatus hernia; or a stomach ulcer. Rarely it is a sign of something more serious such as stomach cancer.

Self-help from the page: cut down on tea, coffee, cola and alcohol; raise your head and shoulders in bed; lose weight if overweight; avoid eating 3 to 4 hours before bed; avoid rich, spicy or fatty food; avoid smoking; and ask a pharmacist or GP before taking anti-inflammatory painkillers, which can make it worse. A pharmacist can suggest suitable products, and pregnant women should ask a pharmacist, since indigestion is very common from around 27 weeks.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you keep getting indigestion, have severe pain, have lost a lot of weight without trying, have difficulty swallowing, keep being sick, have iron deficiency anaemia, feel a lump in your stomach, or see blood in vomit or poo. These can be signs of something more serious.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: suggest meal timing and trigger-spotting as ideas, and flag the warning signs. Chest pain can be heart-related, so any chest pain with sweating, breathlessness or spreading pain needs emergency help. Not medical advice.
