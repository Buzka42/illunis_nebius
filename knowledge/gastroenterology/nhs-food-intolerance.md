# Food intolerance versus food allergy

A food intolerance means difficulty digesting a particular food or ingredient. The NHS says it is not usually serious, though eating the food can make you feel unwell. Symptoms typically start a few hours after eating and can last hours or days. The common ones are diarrhoea, bloating, wind and tummy pain. Others include headache, tiredness, nausea, constipation, joint pain or rashes.

A food intolerance is not the same as a food allergy. Allergy involves the immune system and can cause itchy skin, wheezing, or swelling of the lips, face and eyes, and sometimes a life-threatening reaction. Intolerance is generally a digestive problem rather than an immune emergency.

Call emergency services immediately if, after eating, you or someone else has sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue; very fast or difficult breathing, wheezing or a feeling of choking; a tight throat or trouble swallowing; blue, grey or pale skin, tongue or lips; sudden confusion, drowsiness or dizziness; fainting and not waking; or, for a child, being limp, floppy or unresponsive. A swollen, raised or itchy rash may also be present. These can be signs of a serious allergic reaction.

For recurring symptoms, the NHS advises seeing a GP rather than cutting out foods on your own. A food and symptom diary can help the clinician. Eliminating whole food groups without advice can reduce the nutrients you take in. Related conditions such as coeliac disease and lactose intolerance are different and need proper assessment.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: help the user record what they ate and what they felt, without suggesting a cause. If anyone describes allergic-reaction signs, tell them to call emergency services. Not medical advice.
