# Coeliac disease: an immune reaction to gluten

Coeliac disease is a condition in which the immune system attacks the body's own tissue when gluten is eaten. This damages the lining of the small intestine, so nutrients are not absorbed properly. The trigger is gluten, a protein in wheat, barley and rye, found in pasta, cakes, breakfast cereals, most breads, some sauces and ready meals, and most beers. Oats contain no gluten but can be contaminated if processed alongside those cereals.

Symptoms vary. Gut symptoms: diarrhoea, stomach aches, bloating and wind, indigestion and constipation. General symptoms: tiredness from poor nutrient absorption, unintentional weight loss, an itchy rash, fertility problems, nerve damage, and problems with balance and coordination. Children may grow slowly or have delayed puberty.

It affects at least 1 in 100 people in the UK, and many mild cases may go undiagnosed or be mistaken for IBS. It can develop at any age. Risk is higher with type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, Down's syndrome or Turner syndrome, and among first-degree relatives of someone with coeliac disease.

Testing: routine screening is not done in England. Testing is recommended for people with symptoms or an increased risk. A clinician arranges testing, so raise it with a GP rather than self-diagnosing.

Management: a lifelong gluten-free diet, even without noticeable symptoms, because continuing to eat gluten can cause complications such as weak bones, anaemia and, less commonly, some cancers and pregnancy problems. The diet should still be healthy and balanced, and charities such as Coeliac UK offer help.

When to see a clinician: speak to a GP if symptoms like these persist, or if you have a close relative with coeliac disease, before making big changes to your diet.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: suggest speaking to a clinician before starting a gluten-free diet, and avoid assessing symptoms. Not medical advice.
