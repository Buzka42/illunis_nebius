# Exercise in pregnancy

The NHS says exercise is safe for you and your baby in pregnancy. It is important to start any new activity gently, and it is usually fine to keep doing what you already do, with a few things to avoid.

Benefits: staying fit; preparing the body for birth and recovery; lowering the risk of some pregnancy-related conditions such as gestational diabetes and pre-eclampsia; and better sleep, mood and mental health.

How much: the aim is the same as for other adults, 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week with muscle-strengthening on two days. Moderate means you can talk but not sing. Walking, swimming and yoga all count. The time can be split into shorter sessions. If you cannot reach 150 minutes, do what you can, since any activity is better than none. If you are new to exercise, start with about 10 minutes a day and build up. If you have a known health condition, speak to your midwife about what is safe. It is not recommended to start a new vigorous activity such as running during pregnancy.

Later pregnancy: listen to your body and do not push too hard. Brisk walking may be more comfortable than running. Swimming can relieve pressure on muscles and joints. Avoid activities that involve lying flat on your back for more than a few minutes, as it can make you feel dizzy and is not good for the baby.

Safety: tell your instructor you are pregnant; drink water; keep cool; stop if you feel uncomfortable, too hot or unwell. Avoid sports with a risk of falling or being hit on the tummy, such as hockey, rugby or mountain biking; do not exercise on an empty stomach; and do not scuba dive.

When to talk to a clinician: any pain, bleeding, fluid leaking, dizziness, breathlessness or reduced baby movement should prompt contacting a midwife or maternity unit promptly, and anyone unsure about an activity should ask their midwife.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: keep it to general encouragement and defer personal decisions to the midwife. Not medical advice.
