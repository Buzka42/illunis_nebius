# Premenstrual syndrome (PMS)

PMS describes symptoms some people experience in the weeks before a period. It can affect anyone who has periods, and most have it at some point. Symptoms differ from person to person and from month to month. Common ones: mood swings; feeling depressed, irritable, upset, anxious or emotional; tiredness or trouble sleeping; bloating or cramping; breast tenderness; headaches; spotty skin; greasy hair; and changes in appetite or cravings.

Things the NHS suggests trying: regular exercise; a healthy balanced diet, possibly with frequent smaller meals every two to three hours; plenty of sleep; reducing stress with yoga or meditation; pain relief advice from a pharmacist; and keeping a symptom diary for at least two cycles to bring to a GP appointment. Avoid smoking and heavy drinking.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if lifestyle changes have not helped or symptoms affect daily life. A GP may suggest other treatments such as hormonal options, cognitive behavioural therapy or other medicines, and may refer to a specialist (a gynaecologist, psychiatrist or counsellor) if symptoms continue.

Important safety point: a more severe form, premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), can include suicidal feelings. The NHS says to call emergency services or go to A&E if you have PMDD symptoms and are feeling suicidal. In the UK, Samaritans answer at any time on 116 123; elsewhere use your local crisis line.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: a user who tracks mood, energy and sleep may notice a monthly pattern. Offer to reflect it back in neutral terms and suggest sleep, movement and meals as supports. The symptom diary the NHS mentions is exactly what a clinician would find useful. Do not say that a pattern means PMS or PMDD. Not medical advice.
