# The menstrual cycle and periods

A period is the part of the menstrual cycle when bleeding from the vagina occurs for a few days. The NHS says that for most women the cycle is around every 28 days, but anything from every 21 to every 35 days is common. A period lasts 2 to 7 days, usually about 5, and bleeding tends to be heaviest in the first two days. Blood is red when heaviest and can look pink or brown on lighter days. The amount lost is typically about 20 to 90 ml (roughly 1 to 5 tablespoons), though some people bleed more.

Periods usually begin around age 12, and a late start is not usually a worry. Most girls have regular periods by 16 to 18. They continue until menopause, which usually happens between the mid-40s and mid-50s, with 51 being the UK average. Periods do not normally stop suddenly; they often become less frequent over months or years.

Period products include pads, tampons and menstrual cups, each in a range of sizes and absorbencies, with reusable options.

When to see a clinician: bleeding between periods, bleeding after sex, or bleeding after the menopause needs to be checked by a doctor, as it may be caused by infection, changes to the cervix or, rarely, cancer. If you miss a period you could be pregnant. If a pregnancy test is negative and you have missed three periods in a row, see a GP. The page also directs readers to information on heavy, painful, irregular and missed periods. A clinician can advise about delaying a period.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: if a user records mood, energy or sleep, noting changes across the cycle can help them understand patterns, as an observation and only if the user chooses to share cycle information. Cycle data is sensitive and must stay under the user's control. Not medical advice.
