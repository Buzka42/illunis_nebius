# Period pain

Pain or discomfort before or during a period is common. The NHS describes it as tummy pain at the start of or just before a period, painful cramps that can spread to the back and thighs, or a sharp pain or dull ache. It usually lasts up to about three days and can affect daily activities. It happens because the womb tightens during a period, and it is often a normal part of the cycle.

Ways to ease it: a warm bath or shower; a heat pad or wrapped hot-water bottle on the tummy; massaging the tummy and back; gentle exercise such as yoga, swimming, walking or cycling; and pain relief advised by a pharmacist. Cutting down on alcohol and not smoking may also help.

Sometimes painful periods are caused by a condition such as endometriosis or adenomyosis (womb-like tissue growing in other places), fibroids, or pelvic inflammatory disease (an infection of the womb, tubes and ovaries). A newly fitted intrauterine device can cause period pain, particularly in the first 3 to 6 months.

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 if pelvic or period pain is severe or worse than usual and pain relief has not helped.

See a GP if periods become more painful, heavier or irregular; the pain stops you doing usual activities; you have pain during sex, or when peeing or pooing; you bleed between periods; you notice changes in how much you pee or poo; you have a swollen tummy or loss of appetite; or you are losing weight without trying. For severe period pain a GP can discuss treatment options and, where needed, investigations.

Relevance to the training expert: gentle movement often helps and does not need to be forced. Rest on days that feel too hard.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: acknowledge the discomfort, offer comfort ideas, and mention warning signs without attributing the pain to a cause. Not medical advice.
