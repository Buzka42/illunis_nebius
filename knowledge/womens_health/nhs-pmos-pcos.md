# PMOS (formerly known as PCOS)

The NHS page, retrieved at the address of the older polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) page, now calls the condition polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) and notes it used to be called PCOS. It is a hormone condition that can affect hair growth, periods, fertility and mood. There is no cure, but treatments and lifestyle changes can help.

Symptoms usually start at puberty or in young adulthood and continue until menopause, after which some symptoms stop or change. They can be mild, severe or absent. They include irregular periods or long gaps between periods; excess hair growth on the face or body, or hair loss; weight gain or difficulty losing weight; difficulty getting pregnant; depression or anxiety; oily skin and acne; tiredness; and thick, dark patches of skin on the neck or armpits.

Many of these signs are common and can have other causes, so having them does not mean someone has PMOS. The NHS says it is important to have them checked.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you think you might have PMOS or the symptoms affect everyday life, work or relationships. A GP may arrange tests with a reproductive or hormone specialist, which can include blood tests of hormone levels and insulin resistance and an ultrasound scan of the ovaries (done only for people over 18). Treatments, chosen with a clinician, may address periods, fertility, hair growth, acne, weight or tiredness. Surgery is an option for some fertility problems when other approaches have not worked.

Self-help from the page: eat a healthy balanced diet, exercise regularly, work toward a healthy weight if overweight, try talking therapy if symptoms contribute to low mood or anxiety, and use hair-removal methods if you wish.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: link regular movement, balanced meals and sleep to general wellbeing, and encourage a clinician visit for irregular periods or other persistent signs. Do not suggest medicines. Not medical advice.
