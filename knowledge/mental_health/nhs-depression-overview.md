# Depression in adults: what it is and when to seek help

The NHS describes depression as more than feeling unhappy or fed up for a few days. It means feeling persistently sad for weeks or months. It is a real illness with real symptoms, not a weakness, and not something to "snap out of". With the right treatment and support, most people can make a full recovery.

How it can show up: lasting unhappiness or hopelessness, losing interest in things once enjoyed, tearfulness, and often anxiety. Physical signs can include constant tiredness, poor sleep, loss of appetite or sex drive, and aches and pains. Severity ranges from mild to severe; severe depression can bring thoughts that life is not worth living. Low mood during hard times often lifts after a short period, which is different from depression.

Triggers: sometimes there is one, such as bereavement, job loss or giving birth, and sometimes there is not. Risk is influenced by many factors including family history.

When to see a clinician: the page says it is important to see a GP if you think you may be depressed, and not to delay, since earlier help means earlier recovery.

Treatment overview (decided with a clinician): for mild depression a doctor may suggest "watchful waiting", lifestyle measures such as exercise, guided self-help, or a talking therapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy. For moderate to severe depression a combination of talking therapy and medicine is often recommended, and severe depression may involve a specialist team. Many people benefit from more exercise, less alcohol, stopping smoking, eating well, a self-help book or a support group.

If you are in immediate danger or thinking about ending your life, contact local emergency services or a crisis line now. In the UK, Samaritans answer at any time on 116 123.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: reflect mood trends from check-ins as observations ("your mood ratings have been lower for two weeks"), never as a diagnosis. Encourage speaking to a GP or talking therapy service, and offer connection and gentle activity as supports, not substitutes. Not medical advice.
