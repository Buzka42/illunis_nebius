# Seasonal affective disorder: low mood with the seasons

Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a type of depression that usually occurs in winter and eases in summer. Less commonly, some people have symptoms in summer instead.

Symptoms: low mood; losing interest in usual pleasures; feeling guilty, hopeless or worthless; restlessness or irritability; difficulty concentrating; eating more than usual; feeling more tired and sleeping more; and thoughts of suicide or self-harm. Some people feel unusually energetic at the start of spring or summer.

Cause: not exactly known, but thought to link to reduced daylight in winter, which may change brain chemicals such as melatonin and serotonin that affect mood and the sleep-wake rhythm.

Diagnosis: a GP asks how mood changes over time and may use a questionnaire. A diagnosis is considered if depression symptoms have occurred at a particular time of year for two years or longer.

Treatment options discussed with a clinician include talking therapies such as CBT, medicine, and light therapy using a bright light box that simulates daylight. The NHS says light therapy is not usually offered on the NHS because evidence on effectiveness is limited, though many people find it helpful. The page advises speaking to a GP before trying light therapy because it may not suit people with some eye conditions or on certain medicines.

Self-care: get as much natural daylight as possible by going outside in the daytime; exercise regularly; stay in touch with friends and family; keep up usual activities; keep regular sleep and wake times; limit alcohol and avoid recreational drugs; and avoid phones and screens right before bed.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you think you might have SAD, or if you have it and treatment is not helping. Adults can self-refer to NHS talking therapies. If you are in immediate danger or thinking about ending your life, contact local emergency services or a crisis line now. In the UK, Samaritans answer at any time on 116 123.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: link morning light and routine to sleep and mood as ideas, particularly as autumn arrives. Not medical advice.
