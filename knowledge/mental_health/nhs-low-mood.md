# Low mood: everyday things that help

Everyone feels low from time to time. Low mood is common after distressing events or big changes, and sometimes arrives for no obvious reason. The NHS says it often passes after a few days or weeks, and small, everyday changes usually help.

Signs: stopping activities you like, withdrawing from loved ones, trouble sleeping, and feeling sad, worried, tired, less confident or irritable. A low mood should lift after a few days. If it lasts longer than about two weeks, or you no longer get pleasure from things for most of the day over several weeks, it may be a sign of depression. Some physical illnesses and medicines can also affect mood, which is why a clinician should rule those out.

Practical tips from the page:

- Try self-help CBT techniques for worries and unhelpful thoughts.
- Increase helpful activity: list important or enjoyable things and do some daily, starting with easy ones.
- Talk to someone: friends, family, colleagues or a helpline. Staying connected matters.
- Improve sleep and routine, because low mood and tiredness feed each other.
- Use music: start with slower tracks and move to more uplifting ones.
- Add a little activity every day, such as taking the stairs or stretching after sitting.

If you are having thoughts that life is not worth living, or you are self-harming or thinking about it, tell someone. Urgent help is available. If you are in immediate danger or thinking about ending your life, contact local emergency services or a crisis line now. In the UK, Samaritans answer at any time on 116 123.

When to see a clinician: if low mood persists beyond about two weeks, affects daily life, or you are worried, speak to a GP or an NHS talking therapies service.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: it can suggest one small, achievable activity or a message to a friend, and notice sleep and activity patterns. It should not diagnose or minimise. Not medical advice.
