# Exercise as a support for low mood and depression

The NHS says regular exercise can boost mood if you have depression, and is especially useful for people with less severe depression. Any type of exercise is useful as long as it suits you and you do enough of it. It should be something you enjoy, or motivation will be hard to find.

How much: adults are advised to do 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week. If you have not exercised for a while, start gradually and build toward that. Any exercise is better than none, and even a brisk 10-minute walk can clear the mind and help you relax.

Getting started: find an activity you can do regularly. Options include a team sport, classes at a leisure centre, or building more walking or cycling into daily life. Outdoor options mentioned include Green Gym conservation projects and walking groups that support people with health or mental health conditions.

Exercise on prescription: if you have not exercised for a long time or are worried about the effect on your body, ask a GP about exercise on prescription. Many GP surgeries offer it for conditions including depression. The page notes that NICE recommends group exercise as an option for less severe depression, usually more than one session a week with a trained practitioner over about 10 weeks.

Other help: many treatments exist, including talking therapies, antidepressants and self-help. The NHS advises seeing a GP if you have been feeling down for a while and do not feel like your usual self; the GP can discuss options and help you decide.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: when a user reports low energy and flat mood, a short walk is a small step to propose, with the user's own choice of timing. Be careful never to imply that exercise alone is enough if the low mood is persistent. If you are in immediate danger or thinking about ending your life, contact local emergency services or a crisis line now. In the UK, Samaritans answer at any time on 116 123.

Not medical advice.
