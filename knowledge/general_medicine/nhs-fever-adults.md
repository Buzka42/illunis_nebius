# Fever in adults

A high temperature (fever) is usually a symptom of infection, and the NHS says it often improves on its own and can be managed at home. Normal body temperature differs from person to person and changes during the day. A high temperature is usually considered to be 38 degrees C or above.

You may have one if your chest or back feels hotter than usual; you feel hot, cold or shivery, sweaty, or have warm, red skin (harder to see on brown or black skin); or a thermometer reads 38 or higher. You can feel feverish even if the thermometer reads lower. A thermometer is optional. If you use one, the NHS recommends a digital thermometer under the tongue or in the armpit (following the instructions), and says not to use glass thermometers, some infrared thermometers or forehead strips, because of safety or accuracy concerns.

Home care: rest, drink plenty of fluids (water is best) so your pee is light yellow and clear, and ask a pharmacist about pain or fever relief if you feel uncomfortable. Try to stay at home and avoid contact with others until you no longer have a high temperature.

Causes: many things, often just the body fighting an infection such as a cold or flu. It is usually not serious, but a very high temperature, or one that will not come down, can signal something more serious.

When to get help: ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 if you have a high temperature and it has not improved, or is getting worse, despite treating it at home. Fever together with other warning signs, for example confusion, a stiff neck, trouble breathing, a non-fading rash or severe pain, needs urgent assessment (see the sepsis note and the 999 note). The page is for adults; children and babies have separate advice.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: a wearable's skin-temperature or heart-rate rise can accompany illness, but the assistant should not interpret it. Encourage rest and fluids and flag the warning signs. Not medical advice.
