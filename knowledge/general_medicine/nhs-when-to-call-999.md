# When to call 999 (or the local emergency number)

The NHS says 999 is for life-threatening emergencies, such as serious road traffic accidents, strokes and heart attacks. If you are not sure whether you need an ambulance, NHS 111 can check your symptoms and tell you what to do; for a child under five you can call 111 to speak to someone.

What happens when you call: a call handler asks for the location (postcode or landmarks), what has happened and the main symptom, and your contact number in case they need to call back. They tell you when they have enough information. If you are told to expect a callback, make sure someone can answer. Calling 999 does not always mean an ambulance will be sent; you may be told it is safe to make your own way to A&E or to be seen elsewhere. In a life-threatening emergency paramedics or a community first responder will be sent, possibly in an ambulance, rapid response vehicle, motorbike, their own car or a cycle unit. The handler gives an idea of waiting time and may give first-aid instructions to follow.

How you can help the crew: stay with the person until help arrives; turn on lights and open curtains in a dark house; use hazard lights if you are in a car; call again if the person's condition or your location changes; lock away pets; and, if you can, write down the person's GP details, collect their medicines and tell paramedics about allergies.

Other notes in this bank list emergency signs for particular situations, including heart attack, stroke, severe breathing difficulty, sepsis and anaphylaxis-type reactions. Outside the UK the emergency number differs (for example 112 across the EU), so a user's location matters.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: if a user describes an emergency, the assistant should first tell them to call the local emergency number, then stop giving other advice. Not medical advice.
