# Sepsis: a rare but urgent emergency

Sepsis is a serious reaction to an infection that can be life-threatening. The NHS says it develops very quickly, usually over hours, and needs urgent hospital treatment. If untreated it can progress to septic shock.

Symptoms in adults: confusion or slurred speech; uncontrollable shivering; muscle pain; difficulty breathing; blue, pale, grey or blotchy skin, lips or tongue (on black or brown skin look at palms and soles); a high or low temperature (a high one is less common in older adults); and not peeing all day, or very little in the past 18 hours.

In babies and children: breathing very fast or with difficulty (grunting, or the tummy sucking under the ribs); a fit; a high or low temperature, feeling very hot or cold to touch; blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin; a rash that does not fade when pressed (rare in sepsis but can signal meningitis); being sleepier than normal or hard to wake; a weak, high-pitched or continuous cry; and not peeing for 12 hours. Under-fives may not feed or may keep vomiting.

Symptoms can be harder to notice in babies and young children, people with dementia or a learning disability, and people who have difficulty communicating. The page says to trust your instinct.

What to do: call emergency services or go to A&E for a baby or child under five with the signs above, or if you think an adult could have sepsis. Call NHS 111 if you or your child had sepsis in the past 12 months and now have signs of infection, such as a very high temperature or feeling hot, cold or shivery. Do not wait to see if it settles, and do not drive yourself.

Cause: the immune system overreacts to an infection and starts to damage organs. Risk depends on the infection type, age and other factors.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: if someone describes confusion with fever or shivering after an infection, say plainly to call emergency services now. Not medical advice.
