# Dehydration

Dehydration means the body loses more fluid than it takes in. The NHS notes that if untreated it can get worse and become serious, and that babies, children and older adults are more at risk.

Symptoms in adults and children: thirst; headache and light-headedness; dark yellow, strong-smelling pee; peeing less often; dizziness; tiredness; and a dry mouth, lips and tongue; sunken eyes. In babies: a sunken soft spot on the head, sunken eyes, few or no tears, fewer wet nappies, and drowsiness or irritability.

Dehydration is more likely if you have diabetes, have been sick or had diarrhoea, have been too long in the sun or heat, have drunk too much alcohol, sweat heavily after exercise, have a high temperature, take medicines that make you pee more, or rely on others for drinks.

What to do: drink fluids. If you feel sick, start with small sips and build up. Avoid caffeinated drinks and alcohol, which dehydrate. A pharmacist can suggest rehydration products if you are losing a lot of fluid through sickness or diarrhoea.

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 if you feel unusually tired (or a child seems drowsy); you feel dizzy on standing and it does not pass; pee is dark or scant (or a baby has fewer wet nappies); you or a child breathe quickly or have a fast heart rate; a baby or child has few or no tears; or a baby's soft spot sinks inward. These can be signs of serious dehydration.

Call emergency services or go to A&E if signs of dehydration come with blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue; cold skin; difficulty breathing or very quick breaths; confusion; or being sleepier than normal or hard to wake. These could be signs of shock. Do not drive yourself.
