# Shoulder pain: stay gently active

The NHS says you can usually ease shoulder pain yourself, and to get medical help if it does not start to feel better after two weeks. Recovery can take six months or longer, and it often takes about two weeks of care before it begins to ease.

Helpful habits: stay active and gently move the shoulder; try shoulder exercises for 6 to 8 weeks to stop pain returning; stand up straight with shoulders down and gently back; sit with a cushion behind the lower back; rest the arm on a cushion in your lap; and use pain relief advice from a pharmacist, plus heat or cold packs (a wrapped bag of frozen peas or a wrapped hot-water bottle for up to about 20 minutes).

Things to avoid: stopping use of the shoulder altogether, which can slow recovery; doing things that clearly make it worse; inventing strenuous exercises or using heavy gym equipment; and slouching, rolling the shoulders or pushing the neck forward when sitting. Videos from the Chartered Society of Physiotherapy are suggested for exercises.

See a GP if the pain is getting worse or has not improved after two weeks, or if it is very difficult to move your arm or shoulder.

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 if you have sudden or very bad shoulder pain, cannot move your arm, the shoulder has changed shape or is badly swollen, you have pins and needles that do not go away or no feeling in the arm or shoulder, the area is hot or cold to touch, the pain began after an injury such as a fall, you have severe pain in both shoulders, or you feel feverish or unwell. These can be signs of something serious such as a broken or dislocated bone or a torn ligament or tendon.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: encourage gentle movement and posture breaks. Pain with chest pressure, sweating or breathlessness needs emergency help. Not medical advice.
