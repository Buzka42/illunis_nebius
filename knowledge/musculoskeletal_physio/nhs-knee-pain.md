# Knee pain: first steps and when to get checked

Knee pain can often be treated at home, and the NHS says you should start to feel better within a few days. Call NHS 111 if the pain is very bad.

First steps: put as little weight as possible on the knee and avoid standing for long; apply an ice pack or wrapped bag of frozen peas for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours; and ask a pharmacist about pain relief.

Possible causes, which the page says should not be self-diagnosed: sprains and strains after overstretching, overuse or twisting during exercise; tendonitis, with pain between the kneecap and shin, often from repetitive running or jumping; a torn ligament, tendon or cartilage, where the knee may feel unstable, give way or pop; a dislocated kneecap after a collision; and, with no obvious injury, osteoarthritis (pain and stiffness in both knees, more common in older people), bursitis, gout or joint infection, and Osgood-Schlatter's disease in teenagers. A doctor can arrange treatment depending on the cause, which may include physiotherapy or a hospital referral for a scan or specialist care.

See a GP if knee pain does not improve within a few weeks. In many areas you can self-refer to NHS physiotherapy services.

Get advice from NHS 111 now if the knee is very painful; you cannot move it or put weight on it; it is badly swollen or has changed shape; it locks, gives way or clicks painfully (painless clicking is normal); or you have a high temperature, shivers, and redness or heat around the knee, which could mean infection.

How the training expert can help: during recovery, low-impact options such as swimming or cycling may be more comfortable than running, but the choice should follow pain and a clinician's advice. Return to activity should be gradual.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: reflect that a short rest and gentle movement are common first steps, and name the warning signs. It should not diagnose a type of knee injury. Not medical advice.
