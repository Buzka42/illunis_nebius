# Back pain: staying active and knowing the red flags

Back pain, especially in the lower back, is very common. The NHS says it usually improves within a few weeks, though it can last longer or return. The cause is often not obvious, and a common one is a pulled muscle. Sometimes conditions such as a slipped disc, sciatica or ankylosing spondylitis are behind it. Very rarely, it signals a serious problem such as a fracture, cancer or infection.

What helps in general: keep moving and carry on with normal activity as far as you can, rather than resting in bed for long periods. Walking, swimming, yoga and pilates may help, as may simple stretches. If an exercise makes the pain worse, stop and ask a clinician. A pharmacist can advise on pain relief. Heat or cold packs and a supportive posture are common comfort measures.

If pain is severe or not improving, a clinician may suggest physiotherapy, group exercise, manual therapy by a trained therapist, or cognitive behavioural therapy to help cope with pain. Surgery is considered only for particular conditions after other treatments have not helped. In many areas people can refer themselves to NHS community musculoskeletal (MSK) services for physiotherapy.

See a GP if: pain has not improved after a few weeks of home care or stops you doing everyday activities; you are worried or struggling to cope; you have lost weight without trying; there is a lump, swelling or change in shape; it is worse at night or does not ease with rest; it worsens with coughing, sneezing or pooing; or it is in the upper back between the shoulders.

Get urgent help (GP or NHS 111) if you also feel hot, cold, shivery or unwell, or the pain is severe, sudden or worsening quickly.

Call emergency services or go to A&E if you have pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs; loss of feeling around the genitals or anus; changes in bladder or bowel control or sexual function; chest pain; or the pain started after a serious accident.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: encourage gentle movement and mention the warning signs. Not medical advice.
