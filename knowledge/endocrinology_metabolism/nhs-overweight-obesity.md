# Overweight and obesity: risks and a steady approach

The NHS defines overweight and obesity as having more body fat than the body needs, which can lead to serious health problems, but lifestyle changes and treatments can help.

How it is assessed: body mass index (BMI) is the usual starting point, with lower cut-offs for people from South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean backgrounds, who face risks at a lower BMI. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so a muscular person may be misclassified. Waist-to-height ratio helps show fat around the middle, and the general advice is to keep waist size under half of height.

Risks: type 2 diabetes, heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure, some cancers, osteoarthritis and fatty liver disease. Day-to-day, it can cause breathlessness, tiredness, joint and back pain, snoring and low mood. The page says even a modest weight loss, about 5 to 10 percent of body weight, can improve health.

Approach: eat and drink fewer calories than the body uses and move more, eating a balanced diet low in sugary and high-fat foods and rich in fruit and vegetables, cutting down on alcohol, and losing weight steadily. Support, medicines or procedures may be options through a clinician.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you want help with weight loss; if your waist is more than half your height; if you have prediabetes, diabetes, high blood pressure, joint pain, high cholesterol, sleep apnoea or cardiovascular disease; if obesity runs in the family; or if you have or suspect an eating disorder such as binge eating.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: use respectful language, avoid calorie targets and rapid-loss goals, and focus on habits like walking and balanced meals. Anyone who is worried about their relationship with food should be directed to a clinician. Not medical advice.
