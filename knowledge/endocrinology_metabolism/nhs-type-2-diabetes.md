# Type 2 diabetes: what it is and how it shows up

Type 2 diabetes is a common condition in which the body does not make enough insulin, or the insulin it makes does not work properly (insulin resistance). Insulin lets the body use glucose (sugar) for energy, so without enough of it blood glucose rises too high. The NHS says high glucose over time can cause complications including heart disease, kidney disease, stroke and nerve problems. It differs from type 1 diabetes, which is caused by an immune system problem. Type 2 may be preventable, and some people can put it into remission with weight loss and a better diet.

Common symptoms: feeling very tired, peeing more than usual, being thirsty all the time and losing weight without trying. Others: blurred vision, cuts that heal slowly, and itching or repeated thrush. Symptoms usually develop gradually, and many people have none, which is why checks matter. They are similar in adults and children.

Who is at higher risk: people who are white and over 40, or over 25 with a South Asian, Chinese, Black African or Black Caribbean background; people with a parent or sibling with diabetes; people who are overweight and not very active; and people with conditions such as high blood pressure.

Diagnosis is by blood test measuring glucose, with results usually in a few days. Prediabetes means glucose is higher than normal but not high enough for a diabetes diagnosis, and it raises future risk.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you think you or your child may have type 2 diabetes, or if you have a higher risk. If you are 40 to 74 without an existing condition, a free NHS Health Check may flag risk.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: encourage regular activity, balanced eating and routine checks in general terms. It must not interpret glucose readings or suggest that anyone has diabetes. Not medical advice.
