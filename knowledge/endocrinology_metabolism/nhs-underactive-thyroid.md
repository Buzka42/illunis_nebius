# Underactive thyroid: common, slow to show

An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) is when the thyroid gland in the neck does not make enough hormone. The NHS describes it as common and manageable with treatment.

Symptoms may be mild and develop slowly, getting worse over time: extreme tiredness, feeling cold, weight gain, constipation, difficulty concentrating, low mood, dry skin or hair, hair loss, a hoarse voice, and irregular or heavy periods. Many are common to other conditions, so only a check can tell.

Causes: in the UK the main cause is an autoimmune condition, Hashimoto's disease, in which the immune system attacks the gland. It is most common in women, usually starting between 30 and 50, but anyone can be affected. Other causes include other thyroid conditions, surgery or radiotherapy around the neck, treatment for an overactive thyroid, rare iodine deficiency, and some medicines. A family history raises the chance.

Diagnosis: a GP asks about symptoms and family history, may feel the neck, and arranges blood tests for thyroid hormone levels. The GP may refer to a specialist.

Treatment: usually daily replacement of the missing hormone, often for life, with regular blood tests to check it is right for the person. Taking the wrong amount can cause side effects, so changes are for the prescriber.

Risks if untreated: heart and bone problems; in very rare severe cases a life-threatening emergency requiring hospital care. The page also advises telling your GP if you are pregnant or planning pregnancy.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you think you might have an underactive thyroid, since the symptoms are common and need checking.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: persistent tiredness, feeling cold or low mood alongside sluggishness is a reason to suggest a GP check, not a guess at thyroid disease. Not medical advice.
