# Low blood sugar: signs and when it is an emergency

Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia, sometimes called a "hypo") is when blood glucose falls too low. The NHS says it mainly affects people with diabetes who use insulin or certain other diabetes medicines, and it is rare in people without diabetes. It needs prompt treatment to stop it getting worse.

Symptoms can include feeling hungry, dizzy, anxious or irritable; sweating; shaking; tingling lips; palpitations; tiredness or weakness; blurred vision; and confusion. Severe low blood sugar can cause a seizure or loss of consciousness.

For people with diabetes, the care team gives a personal plan for checking and treating a hypo. In general, treatment means taking a quick-acting source of sugar, rechecking, then eating something longer-lasting. If someone is unconscious they must not be given food or drink, because they cannot swallow safely, and they should be put in the recovery position. Emergency services should be called if they are not responding normally, if rescue treatment is not available or does not work quickly, or if they have been drinking alcohol.

Reducing the chance, for people on diabetes medicine, includes following the agreed plan, checking glucose regularly, carrying quick-acting sugar, not skipping or delaying meals, not drinking alcohol without eating, keeping to alcohol guidelines, and making sure family and friends know what to do.

When to see a clinician: people with diabetes who get frequent hypos, severe hypos, night-time hypos, or do not notice the warning signs should speak to their diabetes team. People without diabetes who get shaking, sweating, a fast heartbeat and confusion should see a GP.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: it should not advise on medicine or insulin. For anyone describing these symptoms with diabetes, point to their care plan and, if severe, emergency help. Not medical advice.
