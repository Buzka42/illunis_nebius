# Overactive thyroid: too much thyroid hormone

An overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism, sometimes called thyrotoxicosis) means the thyroid gland, a small butterfly-shaped gland at the front of the neck, makes too much hormone. These hormones affect things like heart rate and body temperature. The NHS says it can affect anyone but is about ten times more common in women than men and typically appears between ages 20 and 40.

Possible symptoms: nervousness, anxiety and irritability; mood swings; difficulty sleeping; persistent tiredness and weakness; sensitivity to heat; swelling in the neck (goitre); an irregular or unusually fast heartbeat (palpitations); trembling or twitching; and weight loss.

Causes include Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition behind about four in five cases; lumps (nodules) on the thyroid that produce extra hormone; and some medicines.

If not treated or well controlled, further problems can occur: eye problems such as irritation, double vision or bulging eyes; pregnancy complications; and, rarely, a sudden, life-threatening flare-up called a thyroid storm.

What a clinician does: asks about symptoms, arranges a blood test of thyroid function, and may arrange more tests to find the cause. Treatments are usually effective and include medicine that reduces hormone production, radioiodine treatment, or surgery. An endocrinologist normally helps choose between them. The choice belongs to the person and their specialist.

When to see a clinician: see a GP if you have symptoms of an overactive thyroid. Seek urgent help for a very fast or irregular heartbeat with chest pain, fainting or breathlessness.

How a wellbeing assistant can use this: a rising resting heart rate, poor sleep and restlessness in wearable data can have many causes and should not be linked to thyroid disease by the assistant. Suggest a GP visit if a cluster of these persists. Not medical advice.
